## the question the course has skipped

Every case so far has taken "this capability was lost" as a settled fact
and asked what kind of loss it was. This week asks the question the course
has been quietly skipping: how would you know a loss claim is even true?

```notes
Name the skip explicitly — students who've been nodding along with weeks
4 through 8 should feel this question land as slightly uncomfortable.
```

---

## two famous claims, both contested

"The secret of Damascus steel was lost" and "Stradivari's varnish can
never be reproduced" are two of the most repeated claims in this genre —
and both are contested by historians and materials scientists who argue
the originals can be substantially explained or matched by other means.
The mystery is partly a story the objects' reputations outgrew, not a
clean case of institutional forgetting.

---

## a standard, not a verdict

That doesn't mean every dramatic loss claim is false. It means "lost" is
a historical assertion that needs the same evidentiary standard as any
other, and a compelling story is not evidence.

```notes
This week's diagram is the one place in the course where the record track
itself is drawn uncertain — point that out before revealing it, so the
room notices the diagram is doing something different this week.
```

## discussion

- What's the difference between "we lost the secret" and "we can't
  currently reproduce it"?
- Collins could test week 2's tacit-knowledge claim by checking whether
  labs could build a working laser. Damascus steel and Stradivari's
  varnish don't offer that kind of test. What does that difference change
  about how confident we can be?
- If a loss claim can't be tested the way Collins tested the laser claim,
  what's left to assess it with — and is "we can't tell" itself a
  legitimate answer this course should be willing to give?

## activity: test a loss claim

**Setup (5 min).** Individually, brainstorm one "we lost the ability
to..." claim you've heard outside this course — about food, craft,
architecture, anything.

**Groups (20 min).** Pick one claim per group. Work out what evidence
would actually settle it, whether that evidence could in principle exist,
and whether it's actually findable.

**Share-back (15 min).** Each group reports the claim, what would settle
it, and whether they still found it convincing after asking that
question.

```notes
Groups will want to relitigate whether the claim is *true* — redirect them
toward what would settle it, which is the actual exercise.
```

---

## debrief

How much of each claim's appeal survived contact with "what evidence
would settle it"? Does the pattern match Damascus steel and Stradivari's
varnish, or differ from them?

---

## wrap-up

"Lost" is a claim that needs evidence like any other historical
assertion, and a good story isn't it. Week 10 turns the method around
again: instead of asking whether a loss claim is true after the fact, what
would evidence of a loss *in progress* even look like?
