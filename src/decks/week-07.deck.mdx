## nobody has to choose this

Lisanne Bainbridge's 1983 paper names a trap that week 6's deliberate
version doesn't cover: nobody has to choose deskilling for it to happen.

```notes
Contrast directly with last week — no beneficiary to point at here, no
chair to sit in and defend the choice. That's what makes this one harder
to argue with.
```

---

## the irony

Automate a task well enough, and the person nominally supervising it
stops practising the underlying skill simply because the automation
rarely needs them to use it. That would be a fair trade if the automation
never failed. Bainbridge's irony is that reliability is exactly what
erodes the operator's skill, and failure is exactly when that skill gets
called on.

---

## automation surprise

This is [automation surprise](/glossary/#automation-surprise) at its
source: not a system doing something unexpected, but a person, asked to
take back over, discovering their own hands don't remember what they used
to.

```notes
Let this land before moving to the diagram — it's the sentence the whole
week is built to explain.
```

## discussion

- Name a piece of automation you trust because it rarely fails. Has that
  reliability reduced your own practice of the underlying skill?
- Why is *reliability specifically* the thing that erodes the skill,
  rather than automation in general?
- Week 6 makes deskilling deliberate; this week makes it a side effect
  nobody intended. If the outcome looks the same from outside an
  organisation, does the distinction matter to anyone but the people who
  caused it?

## activity: audit your own automation

**Setup (5 min).** Individually, list three automated systems you rely on
— navigation, spell-check, autopilot-adjacent tools, anything.

**Pairs (20 min).** Trade lists. For each system, name the underlying
skill it has quietly reduced your partner's practice of, and have them
estimate honestly how they'd do without it tomorrow — push back on
answers that sound too optimistic.

**Share-back (15 min).** Each pair shares their most surprising "I'd do
worse than I thought" moment.

```notes
The self-assessment is the exercise's weak point on purpose — Bainbridge's
argument predicts people underestimate their own decay, so the partner's
job is to be the outside check.
```

---

## debrief

Look across the room's estimates. Was the erosion visible to the person
experiencing it before this exercise, or only visible from outside — the
way Bainbridge's argument would predict?

---

## wrap-up

Reliability, not neglect, is what erodes the skill here — and the person
losing it is usually the last to notice. Week 8 gives you a case where a
lapsed capability was bought back deliberately, with the cost written
down in public.
