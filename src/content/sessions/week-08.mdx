## The case, or the argument

In 2015, the United States Naval Academy reinstated instruction in celestial
navigation, having dropped it from the core curriculum decades earlier on
the reasoning that satellite positioning had made it unnecessary. The
public reporting around the decision is unusually explicit about why:
concern that satellite navigation could be degraded or denied, and a
judgement that the backup skill needed to exist in people, not only in
policy. It is also unusually explicit about the cost of getting the
capability back — new instruction hours, a specific class year to start
with, instructors who had to relearn or newly learn a skill the institution
had spent decades treating as obsolete.

That itemised cost is why this case matters to the course: most of this
syllabus documents a loss. This one documents what buying it back actually
required, in public, with numbers attached — the closest thing the course
has to reading an invoice for [reinstatement](/glossary/#reinstatement)
rather than just an announcement of intent.

## What we do in the seminar

Read the Naval Academy case as a proposal document: what would you have had
to argue, and to whom, to get this funded before satellite denial was a
live concern rather than a hypothetical? Compare that argument to the one
FOGBANK's reinstatement effort had to make after the fact.

## One reading

[Military Times (2015)](/readings/#military-times-2015-navy) — read for the
specifics: which class, how many hours, and what the stated justification
was, not just the headline decision.

## A question left open

FOGBANK (week 4) and the F-1 (week 5) were both reinstated only once the
capability was urgently needed. The Naval Academy reinstated celestial
navigation as a hedge, before the loss was forced. What would it take for
an institution to reinstate something on that basis more often — and is the
Naval Academy case rare because it's hard, or because most institutions
never notice the gap while there's still time to hedge?
