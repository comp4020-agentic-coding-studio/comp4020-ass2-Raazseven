## The case, or the argument

Every case so far has taken "this capability was lost" as a settled fact
and asked what kind of loss it was. This week asks the question the course
has been quietly skipping: how would you know a loss claim is even true?
"The secret of Damascus steel was lost" and "Stradivari's varnish can never
be reproduced" are two of the most repeated claims in this genre, and both
are contested by historians and materials scientists who argue the
originals can be substantially explained or matched by other means — that
the mystery is partly a story the objects' reputations outgrew, not a
clean case of institutional forgetting.

That doesn't mean every dramatic loss claim is false. It means "lost" is a
historical assertion that needs the same evidentiary standard as any other,
and a compelling story is not evidence. This week's diagram is the one
place in the course where the record track itself is drawn uncertain,
because the uncertainty here is prior to any question about capability —
it's about whether the historical record supports the loss claim at all.

## What we do in the seminar

Take a "we lost the ability to..." claim you've heard outside this course —
about food, craft, architecture, anything — and try to find out, in the
seminar, what evidence would actually settle it. Notice how much of the
claim's appeal survives contact with that question.

## One reading

[Collins (1974)](/readings/#collins-1974) — reread with this week's
question in mind: Collins could verify the
[tacit-knowledge](/glossary/#tacit-knowledge) claim by testing whether
labs could build a working laser. Damascus steel and Stradivari's varnish
don't offer that kind of test. What does that difference change?

## A question left open

If a loss claim can't be tested the way Collins tested the laser claim,
what's left to assess it with — and is "we can't tell" itself a legitimate
answer this course should be willing to give?
