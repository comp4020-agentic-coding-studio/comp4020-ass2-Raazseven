## The case, or the argument

Every case so far has read as something an institution lost without
choosing to. This week breaks that pattern. Harry Braverman argued that the
twentieth-century separation of "conception" from "execution" — planning
work in one place, performing it, deskilled, in another — was a management
strategy, not a side effect of machinery. Judgement was moved upward and
skill was narrowed downward on purpose, because a narrower job is cheaper
to staff, easier to supervise, and harder for the worker to bargain with.

That reframes [deskilling](/glossary/#deskilling) as a choice with a
beneficiary, not a regrettable accident nobody intended. It also means "the
capability was lost" and "the capability was given away" can describe the
exact same event from two different chairs in the same building.

## What we do in the seminar

Take one of this course's earlier cases and argue it the other way: was
FOGBANK's or the F-1's lost capability actually somebody's deliberate
trade-off at the time — cost against risk, this quarter against next
decade — rather than a pure accident of neglect? You don't have to
believe the argument to make it well.

## One reading

[Braverman (1974)](/readings/#braverman-1974) — read the argument in full
before deciding whether it applies past the manufacturing-floor cases it
was written from.

## A question left open

If a capability is deliberately narrowed for good reasons at the time — cost,
safety, focus — at what point does "narrowed on purpose" become
indistinguishable, later, from "lost by neglect"?
