## The case, or the argument

Look back across weeks 2 through 9: FOGBANK, the F-1, deskilling,
automation surprise, contested craft losses — every one was noticed after
the fact, when the capability was needed and turned out not to be there
(or, in week 9's case, argued about after the fact with no live capability
to test at all). None of this course's cases is a story about an
institution that caught the gap while there was still time to act on it
cheaply.

This week turns the method around: instead of diagnosing a loss that
already happened, what would an [automation surprise](/glossary/#automation-surprise)
or a FOGBANK-style gap look like while it was still forming, rather than
after it had already cost years and a rebuilding budget? The honest answer
is that this is harder than diagnosis, precisely because the diagnostic
tools this course has built so far — the record track, the capability
track — are read from documents that describe a state, not from a live
signal that a gap is opening.

## What we do in the seminar

For each of weeks 4, 5, 7 and 8, identify the earliest point at which
someone inside the institution plausibly could have noticed the gap
forming, using only information available at that time. Mark it on the
week's diagram as an early rupture point and compare it to where the
institution actually noticed.

## One reading

[Bainbridge (1983)](/readings/#bainbridge-1983) — reread for its practical
suggestions about maintaining operator skill under automation; several are,
in effect, early-detection measures rather than after-the-fact fixes.

## A question left open

If detection is harder than diagnosis, is it because the evidence genuinely
isn't there before the capability is needed, or because nobody is looking
for it — and which of those two is true of your own organisation, if you
have one?
