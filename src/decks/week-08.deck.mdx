## the decision

In 2015, the United States Naval Academy reinstated instruction in
celestial navigation, having dropped it from the core curriculum decades
earlier on the reasoning that satellite positioning had made it
unnecessary. The public reporting is unusually explicit about why: concern
that satellite navigation could be degraded or denied, and a judgement
that the backup skill needed to exist in people, not only in policy.

---

## the itemised cost

The reporting is also unusually explicit about the cost of getting the
capability back — new instruction hours, a specific class year to start
with, instructors who had to relearn or newly learn a skill the
institution had spent decades treating as obsolete.

```notes
This is the one week in the course with a number attached to reinstatement
in public — worth pointing out explicitly, since most of the syllabus only
documents the loss side of this pattern.
```

---

## why this case matters here

Most of this syllabus documents a loss. This one documents what buying it
back actually required, with numbers attached — the closest thing the
course has to reading an invoice for
[reinstatement](/glossary/#reinstatement) rather than just an announcement
of intent.

## discussion

- Read the case as a proposal document: what would you have had to argue,
  and to whom, to get this funded *before* satellite denial was a live
  concern?
- Compare that argument to the one FOGBANK's reinstatement effort had to
  make after the fact.
- FOGBANK and the F-1 were both reinstated only once urgently needed. The
  Naval Academy reinstated as a hedge, before the loss was forced. What
  would it take for institutions to reinstate on that basis more often —
  and is this case rare because it's hard, or because most institutions
  never notice the gap while there's still time?

## activity: write the reinstatement proposal

**Setup (5 min).** Recap the case as if it were a funding proposal, not a
finished decision.

**Groups (20 min).** Draft the pitch you'd have had to make, years
before any denial event, to a budget committee: who's the audience, what's
the ask, what's the resistance you'd expect.

**Share-back (15 min).** Compare your pre-emptive pitch to what FOGBANK's
after-the-fact reinstatement ask must have looked like.

```notes
Push groups to name the *resistance* specifically — "why spend money on a
backup for something that's never failed" is the actual objection a
pre-emptive pitch has to answer, and it's a harder one than a reactive
pitch faces.
```

---

## debrief

What's structurally harder about the pre-emptive pitch than the reactive
one? Does the Naval Academy's itemised cost make a future pre-emptive ask
easier to argue elsewhere, or is each institution starting from zero?

---

## wrap-up

Reinstatement has a price, and this is the rare case where the invoice is
public before the crisis, not after it. Week 9 asks a question this whole
syllabus has been quietly skipping: how would you know a loss claim is
even true?
