## breaking the pattern

Every case so far has read as something an institution lost without
choosing to. This week breaks that pattern on purpose.

```notes
Name explicitly that this is a deliberate pivot in the syllabus, not a new
unrelated topic — the room should expect the earlier cases to come back
under a harsher light.
```

---

## Braverman's argument

Harry Braverman argued that the twentieth-century separation of
"conception" from "execution" — planning work in one place, performing
it, deskilled, in another — was a management strategy, not a side effect
of machinery. Judgement was moved upward and skill was narrowed downward
on purpose: a narrower job is cheaper to staff, easier to supervise, and
harder for the worker to bargain with.

---

## the same event, two chairs

That reframes [deskilling](/glossary/#deskilling) as a choice with a
beneficiary, not a regrettable accident nobody intended. "The capability
was lost" and "the capability was given away" can describe the exact same
event from two different chairs in the same building.

```notes
Ask directly: whose chair does the room usually sit in when it describes
a loss like this — the one who lost the skill, or the one who narrowed
the job? Most people default to the first without noticing.
```

## discussion

- Who benefits when judgement is moved upward and skill is narrowed
  downward?
- Can "lost" and "given away" describe the same event from two chairs in
  the same building? Which chair are you usually in when you describe a
  loss?
- Take one of this course's earlier cases: was it actually somebody's
  deliberate trade-off at the time, rather than a pure accident of
  neglect?
- If a capability is deliberately narrowed for good reasons at the time —
  cost, safety, focus — at what point does "narrowed on purpose" become
  indistinguishable, later, from "lost by neglect"?

## activity: argue it was a trade-off

**Setup (5 min).** In groups, pick FOGBANK (week 4) or the F-1 (week 5).

**Groups (20 min).** Build the strongest case you can that the loss was
actually somebody's deliberate trade-off at the time — cost against risk,
this quarter against next decade — rather than a pure accident of
neglect. You don't have to believe the argument to make it well.

**Share-back (15 min).** Present the trade-off argument. The class votes,
by show of hands, on how convincing it was.

```notes
Remind groups explicitly that conviction isn't the point — a well-built
argument they don't personally believe is a better outcome here than a
weak one they do.
```

---

## debrief

Does making the deliberate-trade-off argument well change how
"accidental" those earlier cases still feel? What would evidence of
deliberateness actually look like, as opposed to evidence of neglect?

---

## wrap-up

Some capabilities are lost by choice, not by accident — and the same
event can be described honestly from two different chairs. Week 7 gives
you the mirror case: deskilling nobody had to choose for it to happen at
all.
