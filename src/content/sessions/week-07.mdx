## The case, or the argument

Lisanne Bainbridge's 1983 paper names a trap that week 6's deliberate
version doesn't cover: nobody has to choose deskilling for it to happen.
Automate a task well enough, and the person nominally supervising it stops
practising the underlying skill simply because the automation rarely needs
them to use it. That would be a fair trade if the automation never failed.
Bainbridge's irony is that reliability is exactly what erodes the operator's
skill, and failure is exactly when that skill gets called on — meaning the
automation is least trustworthy at covering for its own rare failures,
because the human backup has had the least practice at precisely that
moment.

This is [automation surprise](/glossary/#automation-surprise) at its
source: not a system doing something unexpected, but a person, asked to
take back over, discovering their own hands don't remember what they used
to.

## What we do in the seminar

List three automated systems you rely on (navigation, spell-check,
autopilot-adjacent tools, anything). For each, name the underlying skill it
has quietly reduced your practice of, and estimate honestly how you'd do
without it tomorrow.

## One reading

[Bainbridge (1983)](/readings/#bainbridge-1983) — six pages, read closely;
almost every later citation of "ironies of automation" is citing this paper
specifically.

## A question left open

Week 6 makes deskilling deliberate; this week makes it a side effect of
reliability nobody intended. If the outcome looks the same from outside an
organisation, does the distinction matter to anyone but the people who
caused it?
