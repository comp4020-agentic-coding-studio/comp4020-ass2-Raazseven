## The case, or the argument

An organisation can hold every drawing, every specification, every report it
ever produced about how it once did something, and still be unable to do that
thing. That sentence sounds like a contradiction the first time you hear it.
It isn't one — it's just two different facts wearing the same word.

"We know how we did it" can mean "we have the file," or it can mean "we could
do it again tomorrow." Most institutions, most of the time, cannot tell you
which one they mean, because nobody has ever had reason to ask until the
capability is needed and turns out not to be there.

This course is a method for telling the two apart, tested against eleven
cases where an institution found out the hard way. Nothing here is about
your own habits or your own memory — the subject is [institutional
memory](/glossary/#institutional-memory), at the scale of an organisation,
argued from documented cases rather than from personal experience.

## What we do in the seminar

Read the syllabus against itself: for each of the eleven cases ahead, guess
in one sentence whether you expect "documentation problem" or "capability
problem," before you've read anything about it. Keep the guesses — week 12
asks whether the guessing got easier.

## One reading

[Polanyi (1958)](/readings/#polanyi-1958) — the source of the distinction
this whole course rests on, read before any case so the cases can be read
against it rather than the other way round.

## A question left open

If a [capability](/glossary/#capability) and its record can come apart, what
would it take to notice the gap *before* you need the capability and find it
isn't there? Week 10 comes back to this directly; for now, just sit with the
fact that eleven of the twelve cases in this course were noticed too late.
